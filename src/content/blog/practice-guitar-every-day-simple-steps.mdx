## The One-Hour Practice Split

After building Riff Quest around thousands of logged practice sessions, the routine that sticks for guitarists at every level breaks into four blocks: warm-up, a technique challenge, ear training, and repertoire. For one hour, that means ten minutes of warm-up, twenty minutes of a technique challenge, fifteen minutes of ear training, and fifteen minutes of repertoire plus logging. Warm-up drills come first while the fingers are still fresh, a challenge piece pushed to the edge of current ability comes second before fatigue sets in, ear training and interval work come third once the hands are loose and focus can shift to listening, and songs already known well close the session on something enjoyable instead of something graded. The order matters more than the total minutes — swap ear training and technique, and most players either rush the ear work or run out of focus before the hardest drill of the day. For how this compares across skill levels — beginners need a shorter version, advanced players a longer one — see [how long you should practice guitar daily](/blog/how-long-practice-guitar-daily).

## Warm-Up and the Technique Challenge

The chromatic 1-2-3-4 drill and the Spider Exercise remain the two most reliable warm-ups for isolating finger independence before a session gets demanding: index finger on fret one, middle on fret two, ring on fret three, pinky on fret four, moving string to string at a slow, even tempo. I built more than 30 Spider Exercise variations into Riff Quest's technique library, including all 24 permutations of the 1-2-3-4 finger pattern, so a ten-minute warm-up never repeats the exact same drill two sessions in a row. Five minutes of chromatic work followed by five minutes of a rotating Spider Exercise variation covers the full warm-up block without feeling repetitive.

The technique challenge works best on one phrase, lick, or passage that sits right at the edge of current ability — not comfortable, not impossible. Starting at a slow, clean tempo and repeating the phrase until it plays without hesitation, then raising the metronome by five beats per minute only after three clean repetitions in a row, builds speed without baking in mistakes. Recording the attempt on a phone and listening back afterward catches timing and fretting-hand issues that are nearly impossible to hear while actually playing, since attention is split between the notes and the technique. A genuinely challenging phrase can take weeks of repeated twenty-minute sessions before it locks in at full speed, and that is normal, not a sign of slow progress. This is the same incremental-tempo method broken down in more detail in our [guide to practicing guitar scales effectively](/blog/how-to-practice-guitar-scales-effectively), applied here to a single phrase instead of a full scale.

## Ear Training and Theory

Singing the target pitch before playing it is the fastest way to close the gap between the ear and the fretboard, since it forces the brain to commit to a note before the fingers find it rather than the other way around. I built Riff Quest's Sing What You Play exercise around exactly that sequence — sing a short phrase first, then locate and play those exact notes on the neck — and the Interval Hunt exercise runs a related drill in real time: a root note and one of eight intervals, from a major 2nd to a minor 7th, appear on screen, Pitch Detect confirms the answer the moment it is played, and a fresh interval loads every 12 seconds. Fifteen minutes split evenly between singing intervals and hunting them on the fretboard builds both recognition and physical recall in the same session, a combination ear training alone rarely covers.

Theory fits into the same fifteen-minute block without needing a separate session. Picking one chord progression and targeting only its chord tones — root, third, fifth, seventh — while improvising over it teaches harmony and trains the ear to hear it at the same time, instead of treating fretboard knowledge and listening as two separate skills. Riff Quest's Chord Tone Improvisation exercise runs this drill over a looping backing track and sits in the same theory category as Interval Hunt, so the two exercises reinforce each other inside one practice block rather than competing for separate time.

## Repertoire and Avoiding Burnout

Burnout, not a lack of talent, is the most common reason a practice routine falls apart after a few weeks, and the fix is almost always the same: end every session on a song already known well enough to enjoy without a metronome or a checklist. That fifteen-minute repertoire block is also where most of the motivation to come back tomorrow gets built, since it is the one part of the hour that feels like playing rather than training. On days when even the fifteen-minute version feels like too much, five minutes of a favorite song beats skipping the guitar entirely — consistency across months matters more than the length of any single session. If the repertoire block is running dry, [browse songs by difficulty](/blog/find-guitar-songs-difficulty) to find the next one instead of defaulting to the same three songs every day.

## FAQs

### What should I practice if I only have 15 minutes?

If you've only got 15 minutes to practice guitar, make every second count with a focused plan. Start with a quick warm-up to get your fingers moving - this could be simple finger exercises or a few scales. Then, dedicate a few minutes to practicing chord changes or specific scales that challenge you. Use the rest of your time to work on strumming patterns or riffs, preferably with a metronome to keep your timing sharp. A routine like this packs a lot of progress into a short session.

### How do I know when to speed up with a metronome?

Start practicing at a slow tempo - around **60 BPM** is a good starting point. Focus on playing the exercise perfectly three times in a row before increasing the speed. Once you're comfortable, raise the metronome by **5 to 10 BPM** and repeat the process. This gradual method helps you nail accuracy before picking up the pace, setting the foundation for steady and productive practice habits.

### How can I stay consistent when I miss a day?

If you skip a day, don't stress about it - just jump back in where you left off. The key is to stick with a routine that's easy to maintain, even if it's only 10-15 minutes a day. Be open to tweaking your schedule to keep things workable. What truly matters is staying consistent over time, not achieving perfection every single day. Keep your momentum going and don't let small setbacks derail your progress.
