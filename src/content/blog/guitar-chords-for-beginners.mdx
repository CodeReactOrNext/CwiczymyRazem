Start with **Em and Am**, check that each string rings clearly, and practice moving between the two shapes. Then add C, G, D, E, A and Dm as you need them. These eight open guitar chords give you a useful starting vocabulary for rhythm guitar.

This guide includes diagrams, exact finger positions, short exercises and a ten-minute routine. Keep it open beside your guitar: you can play the first exercise before reading the whole page.

## What is a guitar chord?

A chord is a combination of notes heard together. The major and minor chords in this guide each contain three different note names, although some notes repeat across the strings. For example, an E minor chord contains E, G and B; its open shape uses all six strings.

An **open chord** includes at least one string played without fretting it. A **chord shape** is the arrangement of fingers used to play a particular version of a chord. The same chord can have several shapes in different positions on the neck.

You will see major chords written as a letter, such as C or D. A lowercase **m** means minor: Am is A minor, and Em is E minor. The difference comes from the notes inside the chord, not from how hard you strum.

For this lesson, use standard tuning and no capo. The shapes work on both acoustic and electric guitars.

Imagine the guitar standing upright, with its headstock above the diagram.

<StepList steps="Read the six vertical lines as strings::From left to right: low E, A, D, G, B and high e. Low E is string 6; high e is string 1.|Read the horizontal lines as fret boundaries::The thick line at the top is the nut. A dot in the first space means fret 1; the next space means fret 2.|Use the finger number inside each dot::1 is your index finger, 2 your middle finger, 3 your ring finger and 4 your little finger.|Play strings marked “o” open::You pick or strum them without pressing a fret.|Skip strings marked “x”::Start your strum on the lowest string that belongs in the chord." />

The compact code under a diagram follows the same string order. For example, **Am = x02210** means skip low E, play A open, fret D at 2, G at 2 and B at 1, then play high e open. These six characters describe one chord; they are not a sequence of notes to play in time.

Alternative fingerings are possible. Use the fingerings shown here while following this guide so that the movement instructions match the diagrams. If you play left-handed, keep using the printed string names to orient yourself; these diagrams are not mirrored photographs of your hand.

## Eight open guitar chords

Keep this table nearby while practicing. “Lowest string” tells you where to begin a downward strum.

| Chord | Fingering code, low E to high e | Lowest string | Notes in the chord |
|---|---|---|---|
| Em — E minor | `022000` | Low E, string 6 | E, G, B |
| Am — A minor | `x02210` | A, string 5 | A, C, E |
| C — C major | `x32010` | A, string 5 | C, E, G |
| G — G major | `320003` | Low E, string 6 | G, B, D |
| D — D major | `xx0232` | D, string 4 | D, F♯, A |
| E — E major | `022100` | Low E, string 6 | E, G♯, B |
| A — A major | `x02220` | A, string 5 | A, C♯, E |
| Dm — D minor | `xx0231` | D, string 4 | D, F, A |

You do not need to memorize all eight today. Start with two, then introduce another when you can form the first pair without rebuilding the shapes from the diagram every time.

## Make each chord sound clear

Use this check whenever a new chord sounds wrong:

<StepList steps="Confirm the shape::Check fret numbers, fingers and omitted strings.|Pick the playable strings one at a time::Find the specific string causing the problem.|Adjust one thing::Move a fingertip closer to the fret wire toward the body of the guitar, or change its angle so it clears a neighboring string.|Check again with light pressure::Use enough pressure for the note to speak clearly; extra tension makes transitions harder.|Strum only after the individual notes work::This lets you hear the result of the adjustment." />

| What you hear | What to check first |
|---|---|
| A dull click instead of a note | A finger may be touching a string that should ring |
| A buzzing fretted note | Finger position within the fret and sufficient pressure |
| A clean chord with the wrong bass note | Whether the strum starts on the correct string |
| A pause when changing chords | Whether you need a slower tempo or a simpler two-chord drill |
| Problems across several shapes | Tuning and guitar setup, as well as finger placement |

:::important
If playing hurts, stop and adjust rather than pushing through. A teacher can help check your hand position; a guitar technician can check an instrument that is unusually difficult to fret.
:::

## Practice your first chord changes

A transition needs its own practice. Being able to hold two shapes does not automatically mean you can move between them in time.

### Start with Em to Am

<StepList steps="Form Em::Check the strings one at a time.|Release the pressure::Only enough to move comfortably.|Move two fingers::The middle finger from A2 to D2, the ring finger from D2 to G2.|Add the index finger::On B1, which completes Am.|Strum from the A string::Then return to Em and start from low E again." />

Work without a metronome at first. Notice that the starting string changes along with the fretting-hand shape.

### Practice Am to C with two fingers staying in place

<PhotoBlock
  src="/images/blog/guitar-chords/am-to-c-change.svg"
  alt="Am-to-C transition using the same index finger on B1 and middle finger on D2, with the ring finger moving from G2 to A3."
  caption="Two fingers stay, one finger moves: the whole change is the ring finger travelling between G2 and A3."
  ratio="auto"
  width={1200}
  height={790}
/>

With the fingerings in this guide, your index stays on B1 and your middle stays on D2. Move only the ring finger between G2 and A3. Keep the fingers that stay in place relaxed; they do not need to clamp down harder.

Repeat the movement slowly five times. Then pick the five playable strings in each chord. A smaller movement is useful only if the new shape still sounds clear.

### Put the change on a beat

Choose a slow tempo you can follow; **40–60 BPM is a suggested starting range**, not a required level. Count “1, 2, 3, 4.” Play Am on beat 1, let it ring, and change to C on the next beat 1. Repeat.

Once that works, add a downstroke on each beat. Keep the rhythm steady through the transition. If the change repeatedly arrives late, slow the exercise or return to one strum per bar.

Try four bars without a noticeable stop. This is a checkpoint for this lesson, not a universal test of whether you have mastered a chord. When it feels controlled, add a few BPM and repeat. If clarity disappears, return to the previous tempo.

### An optional one-minute check

Set a timer for one minute and alternate between one pair of chords. **Count one change each time you arrive at the next chord and strum it clearly.** Am → C → Am counts as two changes; the starting Am does not count.

Write down the pair, the fingerings used and the number of clean changes. Compare later attempts under the same conditions. This measures one narrow skill; it does not replace playing in rhythm or listening carefully.

## Your ten-minute chord practice plan

Choose Am and C for this example, or substitute a pair you are currently learning.

<SessionTimeline
  title="Ten minutes, four blocks"
  blocks="Check both shapes:2|Rehearse the change:3|Add rhythm:3|Play a progression:2"
/>

| Time | Task | Exactly what to do | Listen for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00–2:00 | Check both shapes | Pick each playable string in Am, then C. Adjust one unclear note at a time. | Clear intended notes; low E omitted |
| 2:00–5:00 | Rehearse the change | Keep index and middle in place. Move the ring finger slowly between G2 and A3. | Both shapes complete before strumming |
| 5:00–8:00 | Add rhythm | Use one downstroke per beat. Change after four beats. Lower the tempo if needed. | An even pulse at the change |
| 8:00–10:00 | Play a progression | Repeat Am \| C \| Am \| C, one bar per chord in 4/4. | The same rhythm through every bar |

The progression is an original practice example, not a transcription of a particular song. You can stay with it across several sessions. When it becomes comfortable, work on another pair such as G–D instead of adding difficulty everywhere at once.

After the ten minutes, save your practice session. If you use the optional one-minute check, do it separately and include that extra minute in your actual practice time.

## Put the chords into music

Once a pair feels comfortable, try the shapes inside a short progression. For example:

**G | D | Em | C**

Play one bar per chord in 4/4. Start with a single downstroke on beat 1. Then try four downstrokes per bar. Listen to the transitions as well as each individual chord.

This is another general practice progression, not a claim that a particular song uses exactly these shapes or this arrangement. When you choose a song from the [Riff Quest song library](/song-library), check its tuning, capo position, chord versions and hardest section. A song can use familiar chords and still have a demanding rhythm.

Work on a short section you can repeat, then use the same practice loop: check the shape, isolate the change, add rhythm and save the session.

## FAQs

### Which guitar chords should I learn first?

Em and Am are a practical first pair for this guide. Add C next to practice a transition with two fingers staying in place. The other shapes here extend your options. There is no single learning order that fits every hand or every song.

### How many chords should I practice in one session?

Two are enough for a focused beginner session. Spend time checking the notes and moving between the shapes. Add another when you can give it attention without losing the clarity of the first pair.

### How long does it take to change chords smoothly?

It varies with the chord pair, fingering, tempo and your starting point. Compare the same short exercise over several sessions. Look for clearer notes and fewer pauses rather than expecting a fixed completion date.

### Should I learn barre chords now?

You can begin with the open shapes here. Barre chords become useful when the music you want to play calls for them. They are not a prerequisite for completing this routine.

### Do these shapes work on electric guitar?

Yes. In standard tuning, the fret positions are the same on acoustic and electric guitars. A clear, relatively unprocessed sound makes it easier to hear whether individual strings are ringing.

### Can I use Riff Quest if I practice away from my computer?

Yes. You can record your practice afterward. Keep enough detail to enter the time accurately, and check the app's current logging options when saving it.

### Does Riff Quest automatically count my clean chord changes?

This guide uses a manual count. Time logging and optional note detection should not be treated as an automatic chord-change assessment. Count the clean transitions yourself if you use the one-minute exercise.

### Are these fingerings the only correct ones?

No. Several chords have common alternative fingerings. The versions here keep the diagrams and transition instructions consistent. A different fingering may suit a particular song or the next chord better.
