**JustinGuitar is the closest Fender Play alternative here for a free lesson path. Riff Quest fits players who want to organise and record practice. Songsterr is for learning from song tabs, while Guitar Pro is for working with tab files.** The last three do not replace a teacher explaining a new technique, so choose by the job you want to change rather than expecting the same course in another interface.

## Quick Comparison: Which Fender Play Alternative Fits?

| Option | Best use | Lessons or material | Access model | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fender Play (reference)** | A guided course package | Video lessons, songs and practice tools | Paid subscription | Check the offer for your region and billing period |
| **JustinGuitar** | Following a teacher through the basics | Website lessons; separate app and paid products | Beginner website lessons are free | Website access and app subscriptions are different |
| **Riff Quest** | Planning sessions and practising exercises | Exercise library and your own imported Guitar Pro files | Free, community-supported | Not a video course or licensed song catalogue |
| **Songsterr** | Finding a tab for a specific song | Song tabs with playback | Free reading/playback; advanced tools in Plus | Tabs tell you what to play, not necessarily how to learn the movement |
| **Guitar Pro** | Editing and practising tab files | Desktop notation and playback software | Paid software; check the current edition and offer | You need suitable files or separately available scores |

For the source behind each access model, see the product sections. A free lesson, a free account and a time-limited subscription trial are different offers; the table does not treat them as interchangeable.

## What Are You Replacing in Fender Play?

Fender Play combines guided video instruction with song-based material. Its current [product page](https://www.fender.com/products/fender-play-subscription) lists Practice Mode, scrolling tablature, a metronome and Feedback Mode. **It would be inaccurate to describe it as video-only.** The US offer displayed when checked was $19.99 monthly or $149.99 annually; the annual price is charged as a year, not as twelve optional monthly payments. Local offers can differ.

Those bundled features make three different switching decisions possible. You may want similar teaching without the subscription, a better way to work on songs outside the course, or help turning what you already know into regular practice. Write down which problem applies before choosing an alternative. Otherwise it is easy to trade a course you rarely open for a tab app you rarely open.

If the current teaching pace, songs and practice tools already work for you, keeping Fender Play is a reasonable choice. Its [course overview](https://www.fender.com/play) shows a progression from first chords to later guitar techniques. There is no advantage in moving simply because another tool is free if rebuilding your routine makes you stop playing.

## 1. JustinGuitar: A Free Website Lesson Path

JustinGuitar is the first option to examine when the important part of Fender Play is having someone explain the next step. The [official store](https://www.justinguitar.com/apps) distinguishes free beginner lessons from paid products. Its [Lessons & Songs app](https://www.justinguitar.com/store/lessons-songs-app) is a separate product; do not assume that installing the app is the same free offer as watching lessons on the website.

**Choose it if:** you want to learn from a teacher, work through material in sequence and repeat a lesson until the explanation makes sense. Compare the teaching on a skill you are currently learning, such as a chord change, rather than watching an impressive advanced demonstration that tells you little about the beginner experience.

**Its limit:** a lesson can explain a movement without confirming that your hands are doing it comfortably. Pause the video, try the exercise, listen and, when useful, record a short attempt. If your question is about pain, posture or an unclear physical movement, changing course software may not answer it; individual feedback from a teacher can be more useful.

You can also keep JustinGuitar for instruction and use a separate routine. Pick one lesson, extract one exercise, then spend the rest of the session applying it. Our [JustinGuitar alternatives comparison](/blog/justinguitar-alternatives) covers when another teaching approach or a different practice tool makes more sense.

## 2. Riff Quest: Exercises, Practice Plans and a Log

Riff Quest is a Fender Play alternative for the practice around a lesson. You can choose exercises, assemble a session and keep a record of the work. Quests and progression give the routine continuity between days. Access is free and community-supported, without a subscription separating the exercise catalogue into paid and unpaid groups.

In supported activities, Pitch Detect compares the guitar signal with the exercise. That is a narrow kind of feedback: it is not a judgement of your tone, phrasing or overall performance. Read the [note-detection explanation](/wiki/note-detection) before treating a high hit percentage as proof that everything sounded good. You can also practise without enabling detection where the activity allows it.

**Choose it if:** you already have a lesson source and the problem is deciding what to practise, doing the repetitions or remembering what happened last time. The [interactive practice walkthrough](/interactive-guitar-practice) shows the exercise workflow, and the [practice planner](/guitar-practice-planner) explains how to arrange a session.

**Its limit:** Riff Quest is not a replacement for Fender's filmed teaching or its song arrangements. Imported Guitar Pro files are material you supply, not access to another service's catalogue. If you need someone to introduce a technique, pair the exercises with an appropriate lesson. A practical starting point is five minutes understanding the task, ten minutes practising it, then a short note about what needs repeating.

## 3. Songsterr: When You Mainly Want the Song Tabs

Songsterr is useful when you know which song you want and need the written part. Its [official description](https://www.songsterr.com/about) explains the tab archive and playback model. Reading a tab is a different task from following a course: the arrangement gives you notes and rhythm, but you still need to choose a playable section and understand the technique.

**Choose it if:** the songs you want sit outside your current lesson path and you are comfortable reading tab. Start with a short passage you can nearly play, rather than opening a difficult solo and measuring the experience by whether you can immediately follow the cursor.

**Its limit:** the [Plus feature list](https://www.songsterr.com/plus) places tools such as playback-speed adjustment, looping and printing in the paid offering. Basic access is not a promise that every practice control is free. Check the exact control you need before making a subscription decision.

Will the available playback controls let you work at a manageable pace? For a wider comparison of tab readers and editors, use our [Songsterr alternatives guide](/blog/songsterr-alternatives).

## 4. Guitar Pro: Work With Your Own Arrangements

Guitar Pro belongs on this list for players who want more control over written material, rather than a different beginner course. The [official features page](https://www.guitar-pro.com/c/10-guitar-pro-new-features) describes score editing, relative playback speed and working with audio alongside a score. It is a paid desktop product, so check the current licence and system requirements before buying.

**Choose it if:** you already use tab files, write your own exercises or want an arrangement you can edit. For example, you might write out the two bars that keep causing trouble and work on a simplified version before returning to the full passage.

**Its limit:** installing an editor does not give you the same instructional path or material as a course subscription. Check where the score comes from and whether it is included in the purchase you are considering. Also distinguish desktop software from a mobile app; do not assume that a feature demonstrated on one is included on every platform.

For a beginner who has not yet learned to read rhythm in tab, this may be more control than the current problem requires. A teacher explaining an exercise can save more time than a more elaborate playback setup. For an experienced player building a practice collection, the same control may be the main reason to choose it.

## How to Compare Them in One Week

Use the same musical task when trying another tool. A familiar chord change, an eight-bar rhythm part or a short scale phrase gives you something comparable. Trying a new app with a new song, a new tuning and a new technique at once makes it hard to tell which part helped or got in the way.

1. **Day one:** write down the task, your available session length and what a successful attempt would sound like.
2. **Days two and three:** use one lesson source and repeat the same exercise. Record a short attempt rather than relying only on memory.
3. **Days four and five:** put the movement into a song section. Notice whether finding, slowing or understanding the passage is the actual obstacle.
4. **At the weekend:** keep the tools that made starting and repeating easier. Drop the ones that mostly added setup.

This is a suggested evaluation method, not a measured learning guarantee. The [daily practice plans](/daily-guitar-practice-plan) and the [15-minute beginner practice routine](/blog/guitar-practice-routine-builder) provide session structures if you need one, and the [printable practice journal](/guitar-practice-journal) gives you a place to keep the comparison consistent.

Before leaving a paid service, write down the lesson names and goals you want to continue. Check any existing billing arrangement separately from installing another app. Moving a routine is easier when the first session in the new setup is already decided; trying to rebuild your whole history before playing can become a project of its own.

## FAQs

### What is the best free Fender Play alternative?

For a beginner looking for lessons, start by comparing JustinGuitar's free website material. For organising exercises and tracking practice, try Riff Quest. Songsterr is useful for tabs, but its advanced player controls are a separate paid offer. These are different jobs, so there is no single winner for every player.

### Fender Play vs JustinGuitar: which should a beginner choose?

Compare an explanation of the same beginner skill and the way the next practice task is presented. Fender offers a paid guided package; JustinGuitar has free website lessons and separate paid products. Prefer the approach you can understand and use consistently, rather than choosing only by the size of the catalogue.

### Does Fender Play listen to your guitar?

Fender currently advertises Feedback Mode. This comparison did not test its accuracy or verify feature availability on every device, and does not claim that Riff Quest detects notes better. Check Fender's current documentation for the activity and platform you intend to use.

### Can I combine a free course with Riff Quest?

Yes, as a manual workflow: learn from the course, choose a relevant exercise, practise and record the session. There is no automatic progress transfer or account integration implied by that suggestion.
