"What's the best guitar practice app?" is three different questions wearing one coat, and the answer changes completely depending on which one you're actually asking:

- **Learning what to play.** You don't know what to work on next and want a structured course to tell you. This is a teaching problem.
- **Practising a song.** You know the song; you need the tab or the playback, slowed down, looped, in the right key. This is a tooling problem.
- **Organising and recording practice.** You know what to do and you do it, but sessions are ad hoc and you have no idea what you've actually spent time on. This is a bookkeeping problem.

Plenty of apps cover more than one of these — a course app with a decent song player covers the first two, and some tab apps keep a history of what you opened. But almost none cover all three well, and picking an app for a problem you don't have is why so many people end up with four installed and none in use.

## Start With the Problem, Not the App

Answer the question you're actually stuck on, then read only that section.

**"I don't know what to practise."** You want a structured course with an opinion about the order of things: JustinGuitar first, because its free path is genuinely complete for early learning. Simply Guitar or Yousician if you'd rather be walked through it with immediate feedback and a scoring layer. Fender Play or Coach Guitar if video-led, song-first learning suits you better than a curriculum.

**"I know the song, I just can't play it yet."** You want tab with playback and tempo control: Ultimate Guitar for breadth of catalogue, Songsterr for transcription quality and isolating one instrument at reduced speed.

**"I practise, but nothing is adding up."** You want planning and a session history: something that lets you decide what today's session contains, then records that it happened. That's what Riff Quest is for, and it's the narrower job of the three.

One learning source plus one organising tool covers most people. You do not need several apps to learn guitar, and a stack you have to maintain is its own reason to stop.

## Apps for Practising Songs: Tabs, Playback and Tempo

Once technique is no longer the bottleneck, song learning drives most progress, and the tab platform decides how efficient that is.

### Ultimate Guitar: Largest Tab and Chord Catalogue

Ultimate Guitar hosts over two million community tabs and chord charts across essentially every genre, usually in several versions at different difficulty levels. Built-in metronome, tuner and transpose let you match a song to your current level or capo position without leaving the app.

The quality variance is real. For popular songs the top-rated tabs are usually accurate; for deeper cuts you may need to cross-reference with Songsterr or your own ear. Jazz players should be especially careful — community submissions often simplify extended chords (9ths, 11ths, 13ths) down to triads.

### Songsterr: Accurate Tabs and Instrument Isolation

Songsterr takes the opposite approach: a smaller catalogue with higher quality control. Tabs play back as MIDI with speed control, looping, and the ability to solo or mute individual instruments — which is exactly what you want when learning one guitar part out of a full band arrangement, or looping four bars at 60% speed.

### Fender Play and Coach Guitar

Both are video-led rather than tab-led, and both are narrower than the entries above, so here is the short version rather than a padded one.

**Fender Play** is a video course built around playing recognisable songs early, organised into short lessons by genre and skill path. It leans on watching and copying rather than on notation, which suits some learners and frustrates others. Access is subscription-only after a free trial; we have not verified a current figure for this update, and Fender runs promotional rates frequently enough that quoting one would mislead.

**Coach Guitar** teaches through video with colour-coded fretboard visuals rather than tab or notation, aimed squarely at beginners who find standard tab off-putting. A limited set of lessons is free; the rest is subscription. Like Fender Play, it is a learning source, not a practice organiser — neither keeps the kind of practice history discussed above, as far as their documentation states.

## Supplementary Tools

Small, cheap, and genuinely worth having — but add them after the main choice, not instead of it.

**Tuning.** GuitarTuna does one thing extremely well: fast, accurate chromatic tuning in standard and alternate tunings, free, reliable in moderate noise. It also bundles a metronome and chord games.

**Metronome.** GuitarTuna's built-in one is enough for beginners. If you need compound meters, subdivisions or programmed tempo changes, Tempo by Frozen Ape and Pro Metronome both cover that and both have free versions.

**Backing tracks.** iReal Pro (one-time purchase, iOS/Android/Mac) generates full band accompaniment from chord charts with adjustable tempo, feel and style, and ships a large library of jazz standards; it is also the standard tool for blues players who want an authentic shuffle to lock into. Loopy Pro is the alternative if you'd rather build your own loops live.

**Ear training.** Tenuto and EarMaster cover intervals, chord quality and dictation through progressive exercises. Functional Ear Trainer teaches scale degrees in the context of a key, which transfers to playing by ear faster than abstract interval drilling. Five to ten minutes a day compounds.

**Genre fit, briefly.** Jazz players are better served by iReal Pro than by either tab platform, because community jazz tabs routinely simplify extended chords. Classical players want notation, not tab — MuseScore or Noteflight. Metal and progressive players want Songsterr, plus the Guitar Pro mobile app if you have .gp transcriptions.

**Hardware, briefly.** Microphone input is fine for tuning and chord practice. For real-time feedback apps, an audio interface (an iRig-class adapter, or a Focusrite Scarlett-class desktop interface) removes ambient noise and latency, and improves note detection on distorted tones. iOS support for third-party interfaces is more consistent than Android's — check your app's documentation before buying.

## A Simple Starting Stack

You need fewer apps than the internet suggests. A workable beginner setup is **one learning source and one organising tool**:

- [ ] Tune up (GuitarTuna, or any tuner — 1 minute)
- [ ] One JustinGuitar lesson, new or revisited (20 minutes)
- [ ] Log the session in Riff Quest: how long, and roughly what it was (1 minute)

That's it. Add a tab app when you have a specific song you want and JustinGuitar doesn't teach it. Add a metronome app when a built-in one stops being enough. Adding tools before you have the problem they solve is how a practice routine becomes an admin task.

The logging step is the one most people skip, and the one that makes a plateau legible: two months in, "I've been practising loads" and "I practised eleven times, almost all technique" are very different pieces of information.

For a longer session structure once 20 minutes isn't enough, see [how to practise guitar every day](/blog/practice-guitar-every-day-simple-steps) and [how long to practise guitar daily](/blog/how-long-practice-guitar-daily).

## Which Is Right for You?

**Complete beginner:** JustinGuitar for structure, a tuner, and a tracker if you want the record. Three months before you need anything else.

**Motivation is the problem:** Yousician's scoring and streaks are genuinely effective at getting you to open the app — check the price in your own country first.

**Learning specific songs:** Ultimate Guitar for breadth, Songsterr for accuracy and isolating parts.

**You want polished real-time feedback and will pay annually:** the Gibson App.

**Household with several learners:** Simply Guitar's profiles.

**You practise consistently but can't see progress:** that's the bookkeeping problem, and Riff Quest's planning and history are built for it. Pair it with the [intermediate guitar practice routine](/intermediate-guitar-practice-routine) if you also want the session written out for you.

The best practice tool is the one you actually open. An app abandoned after a week is worse than a notebook.

## FAQs

### Which guitar app is best for planning practice sessions?

For building the session itself, JustinGuitar's My Practice Assistant and Riff Quest both let you assemble a personalised list of exercises rather than following a fixed lesson order. Riff Quest additionally records the session afterwards; whether JustinGuitar keeps a timed practice history is not something its documentation makes clear. Most course apps don't plan at all — they sequence lessons, which is a different thing.

### Which apps keep a history of my own practice sessions?

Of the apps here, Riff Quest is the one that records your own sessions — how long, when, and how the time split across technique, theory, hearing and creativity — and shows the totals back. Several others track *course progress*, meaning which lessons you completed, which is useful but won't tell you how much you practised last month. Where we couldn't confirm which of the two an app does, the comparison table says "Not confirmed" rather than guessing.

### Can I practise my own songs, or only the app's library?

It depends on the app's model. Tab platforms like Ultimate Guitar and Songsterr are catalogue-based — you get what's transcribed. Riff Quest accepts Guitar Pro file import, so your own transcriptions become practisable material. Course apps are generally closed: you practise the songs in the curriculum.

### Do I need a paid subscription to get value from a guitar practice app?

No. JustinGuitar's free course content covers a first year of learning properly, GuitarTuna's core tuner is free, and Riff Quest's practice tracking and exercise library aren't paywalled. The paid tiers matter most for tab playback (Ultimate Guitar Pro, Songsterr Plus) and for the feedback-driven lesson apps. A free stack that covers lessons, tuning and tracking is entirely realistic.

### What equipment do I need beyond the guitar?

For most apps, nothing — a phone and its microphone. A tuner is free. If you use a real-time feedback app and find it unresponsive or twitchy, an audio interface that plugs your guitar in directly is the fix, and it matters more on a distorted electric tone than on a clean acoustic. It is an upgrade, not a requirement.

### Can you learn guitar with an app alone?

Far further than sceptics expect, for chords, basic theory, fretboard navigation and song learning. What apps handle least well is posture, tension and hand position — the things a teacher spots in five seconds and a microphone never will. Apps as the daily engine, with occasional human feedback to correct what you can't see, is the combination that works.

## Conclusion

Pick by need, not by ranking. Take one structured course, take a tab source if the course doesn't cover the songs you want, and take something that records what you actually did — then stop shopping. For most beginners that's JustinGuitar plus a tracker; for players stuck on specific songs it's a tab platform plus whatever teaches the technique underneath them.

Riff Quest covers the third job: deciding what a session contains, and keeping the record of it afterwards. It's free, it runs in the browser, and it will not tell you that you're improving — only what you did, which turns out to be the more useful of the two.
